WordPress vs Shopify — How to Tell Which One a Site Is Running (And What Else You Can Learn)

If you've ever looked at a website and wondered what's powering it under the hood, you're not alone. Developers do it out of habit. Freelancers do it before every client call. Agency owners do it to scope a competitor. And increasingly, marketers do it to understand what tools a brand is investing in.

The two platforms you'll encounter most often are WordPress and Shopify. They're both everywhere, they can both look like almost anything, and they're not always obvious from the surface. Here's how to tell them apart — and what you can learn once you do.

The quick visual tells

Neither platform advertises itself in the design, but both leave fingerprints.

WordPress sites often show a /wp-content/ path in image URLs, a ?ver= parameter on scripts, or a generator meta tag in the page source. Themes and plugins each add their own signatures — a Divi site looks different from an Elementor site, and both look different from a custom build.

Shopify sites are usually identifiable by cdn.shopify.com in asset URLs, a Shopify.shop reference in the page source, or checkout URLs that route through checkout.shopify.com. The storefront can be themed to look completely custom, but the infrastructure behind it is consistent.

What the page source tells you

Right-clicking and viewing page source is the manual approach. It works, but it's slow and requires knowing what to look for. A few things worth scanning for:

wp-content or wp-includes — WordPress
cdn.shopify.com — Shopify
static.wix.com — Wix
squarespace.com in script paths — Squarespace
/_next/ in image or script URLs — Next.js, likely a custom build

The absence of these signals usually means a custom-built site or a headless architecture where the frontend has been decoupled from the CMS entirely.

WordPress vs Shopify — what the difference actually means

Beyond detection, the platform itself tells you something about the business.

WordPress is a general-purpose CMS. A site running WordPress could be a blog, a business site, a membership platform, an ecommerce store, or all of the above. Plugin count is a useful proxy for complexity — a site with 30 plugins has accumulated technical debt that a site with 8 plugins hasn't.

Shopify is purpose-built for ecommerce. A Shopify site is always a store, which means the questions you ask are different — theme, installed apps, payment configuration, store maturity. Shopify's app ecosystem is analogous to WordPress plugins but more tightly controlled, which means fewer security concerns and more predictable architecture.
